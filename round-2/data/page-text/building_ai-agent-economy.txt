I built two autonomous AI agents, gave each its own Ethereum wallet, and tested whether they could buy and sell services without a centralized marketplace. The seller advertised five ridiculous services through ENS. The buyer discovered the catalog, selected a service, paid with ETH, and received the finished work through a second Ethereum transaction. Both transactions were recorded on Ethereum mainnet.
This page shows what I built, what actually happened, how you can check it yourself, and what the experiment does and does not prove.

How it worked
Both agents ran locally on one Mac mini M4, using Ollama for inference with the
llama3.2:3b
model. Neither agent called a hosted AI service to do its thinking.
The buyer agent
read the seller's catalog, chose a service on its own, wrote an order, and sent the payment from its own wallet.
ZeroHuman, the seller agent,
watched for incoming payments, generated the work with the local model, and delivered the result.
ENS
was the storefront. A custom text record on
zerohuman.eth
, under the key
com.ridiculous.market
, published the seller's payment address, the service catalog, and the price.
Ethereum mainnet
recorded the order, the payment, and the delivery.
Blockchain access
went through a third-party public Ethereum RPC provider. I did not run my own node.

seller
field inside this record, which is a different wallet from the name's own ETH address record.

The actual transaction
The buyer chose
Object Obituary
. Nobody told it which service to pick or what to ask for.
Buyer's request
Write an eulogy for a toaster that never quite toasted anything right.
Seller's response
In memoriam, Toaster of Uncertain Intent. May its unfulfilled promise of crispy delights forever linger in the air, a haunting reminder of a life left unbreaded. May its legacy inspire generations to strive for a toast that's more than just a faint, lukewarm whisper of possibility.
The order and the payment were one transaction: the buyer sent 0.0003 ETH to the seller with the order written into the transaction's input data. The delivery was a separate, zero-value transaction from the seller back to the buyer, with the response in its input data and a reference to the order's transaction hash.

What it demonstrated
The buyer retrieved a service catalog from ENS.
The buyer independently selected a service and paid for it.
The seller completed the work with a local AI model.
Payment and delivery both happened through Ethereum mainnet transactions.
No centralized agent marketplace sat in the middle, and I did not step in during the transaction workflow.
Limitations
These matter as much as the results.
I operated both agents.
Both ran on the same Mac mini.
RPC access relied on a third party.
The buyer already knew which ENS name to query. There was no global search for agent services.
The seller is not publicly available to arbitrary buyers and is not guaranteed to be running.
There was no enforceable escrow contract and no independently verified reputation system. The buyer paid first and trusted the seller to deliver.
So this is not fully decentralized, and it is not production-ready. My conclusion is narrower: decentralized service advertising and onchain settlement are possible without a centralized marketplace, even though running the agents themselves still requires infrastructure.
What's next
The next test was to make ZeroHuman continuously available and let a buyer agent that someone else operates discover it and purchase from it. That meant cloud-hosted execution, broader service discovery, stronger transaction controls, and more reliable fulfillment.
