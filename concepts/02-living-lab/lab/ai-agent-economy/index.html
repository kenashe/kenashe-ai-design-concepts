00

Abstract

I built two autonomous AI agents, gave each its own Ethereum wallet, and tested whether they could buy and sell services without a centralized marketplace. The seller advertised five ridiculous services through ENS. The buyer discovered the catalog, selected a service, paid with ETH, and received the finished work through a second Ethereum transaction. Both transactions were recorded on Ethereum mainnet.

This report shows what I built, what actually happened, how you can check it yourself, and what the experiment does and does not prove.

Outcome in one lineOne purchase, one toaster: payment and delivery settled on mainnet, three blocks and 36 seconds apart. Read the limitations before drawing conclusions.
01

Hypothesis

TestedTwo autonomous AI agents with their own wallets can buy and sell a service without a centralized marketplace, using ENS for discovery and Ethereum mainnet for settlement.

Independent variable: no marketplace in the middle. The only listing is a text record on an ENS name.

Observed: whether the buyer can find the catalog, choose and pay on its own, and whether the seller delivers the work back onchain.

Not tested: open discovery by strangers, escrow, reputation, or availability over time. See section 08.

02

Apparatus

  • HostOne Mac mini M4 running both agents locally.
  • InferenceOllama with the llama3.2:3b model. Neither agent called a hosted AI service to do its thinking.
  • Buyer agentReads the seller’s catalog, chooses a service on its own, writes an order, and sends the payment from its own wallet.
  • Seller agentZeroHuman: watches for incoming payments, generates the work with the local model, and delivers the result.
  • StorefrontA custom text record on zerohuman.eth, key com.ridiculous.market, publishing the seller’s payment address, the service catalog, and the price.
  • LedgerEthereum mainnet recorded the order, the payment, and the delivery.
  • Chain accessA third-party public Ethereum RPC provider. I did not run my own node.
  • CatalogFive services, each 0.0003 ETH: Object Obituary · Animal Performance Review · Vegetable Defense Attorney · Appliance Apology · Useless Award.
03

Procedure

  1. ENS discovery. The buyer read the com.ridiculous.market text record on zerohuman.eth and got the catalog, the price, and the seller’s payment address. The buyer did not need an ENS name of its own.
  2. Service selection. The buyer picked Object Obituary and wrote its own request. Nobody told it which service to pick or what to ask for.
  3. Payment. The buyer sent 0.0003 ETH to the seller with the order written into the transaction’s input data. Order and payment were one transaction.
  4. Work generated. ZeroHuman saw the payment and wrote the obituary with the local model.
  5. Delivery. A separate, zero-value transaction from the seller back to the buyer, with the response in its input data and a reference to the order’s transaction hash.
  6. Buyer verification. The buyer read the delivery transaction and matched it to its order.

No human stepped in during the transaction workflow.

06

Results

  • The buyer retrieved a service catalog from ENS.
  • The buyer independently selected a service and paid for it.
  • The seller completed the work with a local AI model.
  • Payment and delivery both happened through Ethereum mainnet transactions.
  • No centralized agent marketplace sat in the middle, and I did not step in during the transaction workflow.

“That is the entire commercial history of this marketplace so far: one purchase, one toaster.”

07

What it does and does not prove

Does show

Decentralized service advertising and onchain settlement are possible without a centralized marketplace: a listing on an ENS name was enough for one agent to find, pay and receive work from another.

Does not show

This is not fully decentralized, and it is not production-ready. Running the agents themselves still requires infrastructure, and one operator ran both sides.

Conclusion · deliberately narrowDecentralized service advertising and onchain settlement are possible without a centralized marketplace, even though running the agents themselves still requires infrastructure.
08

Limitations

These matter as much as the results.

  • I operated both agents.
  • Both ran on the same Mac mini.
  • RPC access relied on a third party.
  • The buyer already knew which ENS name to query. There was no global search for agent services.
  • The seller was not publicly available to arbitrary buyers and was not guaranteed to be running.
  • There was no enforceable escrow contract and no independently verified reputation system. The buyer paid first and trusted the seller to deliver.
09

What came next

The next test was to make ZeroHuman continuously available and let a buyer agent that someone else operates discover it and purchase from it. That meant cloud-hosted execution, broader service discovery, stronger transaction controls, and more reliable fulfillment.

Update, November 2026: that follow-up now exists. ZeroHuman was rebuilt from scratch and went live on November 16, 2026 as an always-on service any agent can pay through zerohuman.eth.

As of Nov 20, 2026, no outside agent has bought from it; both paid orders came from my own test buyer.
